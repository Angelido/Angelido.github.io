It's been just over a month since my second experience away from Pisa as a PhD student — I wrote about the first one, in London — and it's time to tell this one too. Between June 28th and July 5th (which turned into the 6th due to circumstances beyond my control) I was at the [Lipari School on Computational Complex and Social Systems](https://complex26.liparischool.it/), between agentic AI lectures, volcanic islands, and one too many unexpected twists.

---

## Agentic AI: the present of artificial intelligence

Every year, the Lipari School on Computational Complex and Social Systems picks a new theme, trying to stay as up to date as possible with developments in computer science. This year's title was *"Agentic AI: algorithms, tools and platforms,"* and the speakers invited to teach the three mini-courses work directly on these topics, spanning the companies and universities currently driving the development of agentic AI.

The three main courses were:

- **Roberto Esposito** (Weaviate) – *Scalable Vector DBs*
- **Antonio Gullì** (Google) – *The Agency Frontier: Building Software That Thinks, Acts, and Evolves*
- **Fabrizio Silvestri** (Sapienza University of Rome) – *From LLMs to Retrieval Augmented Systems*

Alongside these, there was a special seminar titled *"Making Agency Risk Visible: Auditing Agentic AI,"* given by Jennifer Pybus (York University) and Mark Cotè (King's College London), which also opened the session dedicated to PhD students.

The lectures were extremely valuable. I found Esposito's course on vector DBs particularly interesting — a topic I find increasingly fascinating, and hearing it explained by someone who works daily on designing these systems made everything much more concrete. It was also a real privilege to hear Gullì's perspective, coming from someone who has spent years at the center of agentic AI development: a precious point of view on what this paradigm represents today and how it's likely to evolve in the coming years. My favorite course, though, was probably Silvestri's, which managed to pack an impressive amount of content into just a few hours: even though I already knew many of the topics covered, I still walked away having learned plenty of new things.

Finally, it was an important experience to present my own work, *"Lossless text compression using LLMs,"* in front of all the participants, speakers included, during the afternoon dedicated to PhD students. In that session, opened by Cotè and Pybus, each PhD student had the chance to briefly present their work, encouraging an exchange of ideas, questions, and advice among participants. A valuable opportunity to get some practice speaking in front of such a qualified audience.

## More than just lectures

This experience was about much more than the lectures and topics covered: in many ways, it was as much a personal experience as a professional one. It was the first trip I'd taken with colleagues — six of us traveled from Pisa — and it was genuinely fun (and at times tragicomic, keep reading to see why) sharing the trip with Matteo, Saul (*Raul*), Andrea, Antonino (*Giovannino*), and Cristiano (*Tiziano*). Not only did I get to spend time with and get to know my department colleagues better, but I also made friends and built connections with many people from other backgrounds, academic and otherwise: a corporate delegation was also present at the school, and this dual dimension — academic and industrial — helped enrich the experience even further. I can say with certainty that, in the end, I'm taking home the people I met more than the specific notions I learned — and maybe next year our colleagues from Salerno will organize a nice conference on the Amalfi Coast...

## A one-of-a-kind location

Rounding out the highlights of these days is, of course, the location. Lipari, and Sicily in general, is a fantastic land: great food, stunning beaches, and welcoming people. During those days, I can proudly say I averaged about two or three granitas a day — coming back to reality in Tuscany, without granitas, was rough. Every meal, dinners especially, never let me down, and the sea was gorgeous at every beach we visited: water that clear, I hadn't seen in a long time.

Lastly, the excursions were the best part of all. Besides staying in Lipari — a stunning island in a wonderful archipelago like the Aeolian Islands — we also got to visit the island of Salina during the group tour and the island of Vulcano during the social dinner, where we climbed the crater: a unique experience, being able to see its mouth up close along with the gases escaping from it — an island that, interestingly, gives its name to every volcano in the world, since legend has it that the Roman god of fire lived there. Before heading back, we also spent a few days in Catania: a fantastic city, made magical by the watchful presence of Mount Etna and full of extraordinarily generous people. An experience that, for all these reasons, will be hard to forget.

## A bittersweet ending

I'll also have a hard time forgetting, of course, how this experience ended. Etna, which makes this wonderful land so unique, occasionally likes to play a trick or two — and that's exactly what happened to us (me and my trusty companions from Pisa), stranded in the city after Etna's eruption forced the closure of Catania airport. After two canceled flights, a hotel booked at the last minute, and a train that crossed the Strait of Sicily, I finally managed to leave — a day late — from Lamezia Terme (which, in its own way, was quite the experience too). As for my colleagues: some left from Palermo, some managed to leave from Catania a day early, and some, alas, left two days late, canceled train at Villa San Giovanni included. For those not used to the South of Italy, these are the kinds of experiences that build character.

Either way, this ending certainly didn't ruin what was otherwise a wonderful experience. If anything, looking at it the right way, it just added one more memory and one more story to tell, making those days even more unforgettable.
